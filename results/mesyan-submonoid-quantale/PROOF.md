# Proof of the Split Resolution of Mesyan's Question 34

**Reference:** Z. Mesyan, *Quantales Arising From Rings*, [arXiv:2611.31109v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31109) (math.RA, 23 Nov 2026), Section 9, Question 34.

## Setup and Reduction (R)

In Mesyan's terminology, a *semiring* is a set $R$ equipped with a commutative monoid operation $(R, +, 0)$ and a semigroup operation $(R, \cdot)$ that distributes over $+$ on both sides and satisfies $0r = r0 = 0$ (no multiplicative identity is assumed). A *domain* is a nonzero semiring with no zero divisors ($rp \ne 0$ for all $r, p \ne 0$, Remark 14). The quantale $\mathcal{M}(R)$ is the set of all additive submonoids of $R$, with multiplication
$$S \cdot T = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^k s_i t_i : k \ge 0,\; s_i \in S,\; t_i \in T \right\}$$
(Definition 4), i.e., the additive submonoid generated by $\{st \mid s \in S, t \in T\}$. For $x \in R$, write $\langle x \rangle = \{nx : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ for the cyclic additive submonoid generated by $x$.

**Lemma (Reduction (R)).** *For any semiring $R$, $\mathcal{M}(R)$ is commutative if and only if $\langle rs \rangle = \langle sr \rangle$ for all $r, s \in R$.*

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) For any $r, s \in R$, $\langle r \rangle \cdot \langle s \rangle = \langle rs \rangle$ (Lemma 21(1)): every element of $\langle r \rangle$ has the form $nr$ and every element of $\langle s \rangle$ has the form $ms$, so $(nr)(ms) = (nm)(rs) \in \langle rs \rangle$, while $rs \in \langle r \rangle \cdot \langle s \rangle$. Commutativity of $\mathcal{M}(R)$ therefore gives $\langle rs \rangle = \langle r \rangle \cdot \langle s \rangle = \langle s \rangle \cdot \langle r \rangle = \langle sr \rangle$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\langle rs \rangle = \langle sr \rangle$ for all $r, s \in R$, and let $S, T \in \mathcal{M}(R)$. For every generator $st$ ($s \in S, t \in T$) of $S \cdot T$, we have $st \in \langle st \rangle = \langle ts \rangle \subseteq T \cdot S$ because $ts \in T \cdot S$. Hence $S \cdot T \subseteq T \cdot S$, and by symmetry $S \cdot T = T \cdot S$. $\square$

## Theorem A (Ring Domains: Affirmative Answer)

**Theorem A.** *Let $R$ be a (possibly nonunital) ring with no zero divisors. Then $\mathcal{M}(R)$ is commutative if and only if $R$ is commutative.*

*Proof.* The implication ($R$ commutative $\Rightarrow$ $\mathcal{M}(R)$ commutative) is immediate from Reduction (R) (and Proposition 5 of Mesyan).

For the converse, assume $\mathcal{M}(R)$ is commutative and suppose for contradiction that there exist $a, b \in R$ with $ab \ne ba$. Then $a \ne 0$ and $b \ne 0$, so $ab \ne 0$ and $ba \ne 0$ since $R$ has no zero divisors.

1. **Prime characteristic.** By Reduction (R), $\langle ab \rangle = \langle ba \rangle$, so there exist integers $n, m \ge 1$ with $ba = n(ab)$ and $ab = m(ba)$. Then $(mn)(ab) = ab$, so $(mn - 1)(ab) = 0$. Since $ab \ne ba$, we have $n \ne 1$, hence $mn \ge 2$ and $mn - 1 \ge 1$.
   Let $p \ge 1$ be the least positive integer such that $p(ab) = 0$. For every $y \in R$, $(ab)(py) = (p(ab))y = 0$, and since $ab \ne 0$ and $R$ has no zero divisors, $py = 0$. Moreover, if $p = uv$ with $u, v \ge 1$, then $(u(ab))(v(ab)) = p((ab)^2) = 0$, so $u(ab) = 0$ or $v(ab) = 0$; by minimality of $p$, $u = p$ or $v = p$. Since $ab \ne 0$, $p \ne 1$, so $p$ is prime, and for any $y \ne 0$ and $z \in \mathbb{Z}$, $zy = 0 \iff p \mid z$.
2. **Proportionality in characteristic $p$.** Write $\lambda = n \bmod p \in \mathbb{F}_p$. Since $ba \ne 0$ and $ba \ne ab$, we have $\lambda \notin \{0, 1\}$. Then
   $$ba^2 = (ba)a = \lambda(ab)a = \lambda a(ba) = \lambda^2 a^2 b.$$
3. **Applying (R) to $(a + a^2, b)$.** By Reduction (R), $b(a + a^2) = \mu(a + a^2)b$ for some $\mu \in \mathbb{F}_p$. Expanding gives
   $$((\lambda - \mu)a + (\lambda^2 - \mu)a^2)b = (\lambda - \mu)ab + (\lambda^2 - \mu)a^2 b = 0.$$
   Since $b \ne 0$ and $R$ has no zero divisors,
   $$(\lambda - \mu)a + (\lambda^2 - \mu)a^2 = 0.$$
4. **Forced quadratic relation.** If $\lambda^2 - \mu = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$, then $(\lambda - \mu)a = 0$, so $\lambda - \mu = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ (as $a \ne 0$), yielding $\lambda^2 = \lambda$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$, contradicting $\lambda \notin \{0, 1\}$. Hence $\lambda^2 - \mu \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$, so
   $$a^2 = ca, \qquad c = (\mu - \lambda)(\lambda^2 - \mu)^{-1} \in \mathbb{F}_p.$$
   Because $a \ne 0$ and $R$ has no zero divisors, $a^2 \ne 0$, so $c \ne 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$.
5. **Nonzero idempotent forces centrality.** Set $e = c^{-1} a \in R$. Then $e \ne 0$ and $e^2 = c^{-2} a^2 = c^{-1} a = e$. For every $y \in R$,
   $$e(ey - y) = e^2 y - ey = 0 \implies ey = y, \qquad (ye - y)e = ye^2 - ye = 0 \implies ye = y.$$
   Thus $e$ is a two-sided multiplicative identity of $R$, and $a = ce$ is central:
   $$ab = (ce)b = c(eb) = cb = c(be) = b(ce) = ba,$$
   contradicting $ab \ne ba$. $\square$

*Remark.* Theorem A settles all prime-characteristic ring domains ($p \ge 3$), which fall outside Proposition 22(3) of Mesyan because every element satisfies $(p+1)r = r \ne 3r$. Example 6 of Mesyan ($X(\mathbb{F}_3)$) shows that the domain hypothesis cannot be dropped.

## Theorem B (Semiring Domains: 14-Element Counterexample)

**Theorem B.** *There exists a 14-element noncommutative semiring domain $R_{14}$ such that $\mathcal{M}(R_{14})$ is commutative.*

*Construction.* Let $G = \mathbb{Z}_2^2$ and set
$$R_{14} = \{0, \infty\} \cup (\mathbb{Z}_3 \times G), \qquad |R_{14}| = 2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 14.$$
Define the bicharacter $\beta : G \times G \to \mathbb{Z}_3^\times = \{1, 2\}$ by
$$\beta((g_1, g_2), (h_1, h_2)) = (-1)^{g_1 h_2} \in \mathbb{Z}_3^\times.$$
Define addition on $R_{14}$ by taking $0$ to be the additive identity, $\infty + x = x + \infty = \infty$ for all $x \ne 0$, and on $\mathbb{Z}_3 \times G$:
$$(i, g) + (j, h) = \begin{cases} (i + j \bmod 3,\, g) & \text{if } g = h, \\ \infty & \text{if } g \ne h. \end{cases}$$
Define multiplication on $R_{14}$ by taking $0$ to be absorbing ($0x = x0 = 0$), $\infty \cdot x = x \cdot \infty = \infty$ for all $x \ne 0$, and on $\mathbb{Z}_3 \times G$:
$$(i, g) \cdot (j, h) = (ij\beta(g, h) \bmod 3,\; g + h).$$

*Verification.*
1. **Semiring axioms.** Addition is clearly commutative and associative with identity $0$: adding two elements in the same fibre adds their $\mathbb{Z}_3$-coordinates, and any sum involving two distinct fibres or $\infty$ collapses to $\infty$. Multiplication is associative because $\beta$ is biadditive into $\mathbb{Z}_3^\times$, hence satisfies the 2-cocycle identity $\beta(g, h)\beta(g + h, k) = \beta(g, h + k)\beta(h, k)$. Two-sided distributivity holds because for any fixed $g \in G$, translation $h \mapsto g + h$ is a bijection on $G$ (preserving equal vs. distinct fibres), and on each fibre $(i, g) \cdot ((j, h) + (k, h)) = (i(j+k)\beta(g, h), g + h) = (i, g)(j, h) + (i, g)(k, h)$ (and similarly on the right). Zero is absorbing by definition.
2. **Domain property.** No product of two nonzero elements equals $0$: products with $\infty$ equal $\infty$, and products of fibre elements lie in $\mathbb{Z}_3 \times G$, which is disjoint from $\{0\}$. (Note that $(0, g) \in \mathbb{Z}_3 \times G$ is distinct from the additive identity $0$ of $R_{14}$!)
3. **Noncommutativity.** For $a = (1, (1, 0))$ and $b = (1, (0, 1))$,
   $$ab = (1 \cdot 1 \cdot \beta((1,0), (0,1)), (1,1)) = (2, (1, 1)) \ne (1, (1, 1)) = ba.$$
4. **Commutativity of $\mathcal{M}(R_{14})$.** For any $r, s \in R_{14}$, if either is $0$ or $\infty$, then $rs = sr$. If $r = (i, g)$ and $s = (j, h)$, then $rs$ and $sr$ lie in the same fibre $g + h \in G$ and their first coordinates are $ij\beta(g, h)$ and $ij\beta(h, g)$, which either agree (so $rs = sr$) or differ by the unit $2 = -1 \in \mathbb{Z}_3^\times$ (so $rs = 2(sr)$ and $sr = 2(rs)$ in the fibre $\mathbb{Z}_3 \times \{g + h\}$). In both cases $\langle rs \rangle = \langle sr \rangle$. By Reduction (R), $\mathcal{M}(R_{14})$ is commutative. $\square$

*Remark.* The same construction yields a noncommutative semiring domain with commutative submonoid quantale for any modulus $d \ge 3$ and any non-symmetric bicharacter $G \times G \to \mathbb{Z}_d^\times$. Every fibre element $r = (1, g) \in R_{14}$ satisfies $4r = r \ne 3r$, confirming consistency with Proposition 22(3) of Mesyan.
